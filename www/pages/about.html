Project Koios is the personal technical website of Eugene Joseph M. Ragasa.

It is an umbrella for teaching material, research notes, computational models, software notes, finance-oriented analysis, hardware notes, and technical projects that do not need to live in separate fake boxes.

About Project Koios

Project Koios is where I keep technical work that would otherwise end up scattered across too many places: course folders, research notes, computational notebooks, code repositories, private drafts, and half-finished project ideas.

The site is broad because my interests are broad. It is written for people who want to understand things deeply, not for people looking for summaries, shortcuts, or software instructions with the thinking removed.

I connect topics the way I understand them, so the organization may be unorthodox. Real technical work does not always fit cleanly inside course titles, department labels, or website categories.

About Eugene Joseph M. Ragasa

I am an Assistant Professor in the Department of Physics at De La Salle University. My background runs through mathematics, mathematical finance, mechanical engineering, and materials science and engineering.

I earned a B.S. in Mathematics from the United States Military Academy, an M.A. in Mathematical Finance from Columbia University, an M.S. in Engineering Science from the University of the Pacific, and a Ph.D. in Materials Science and Engineering from the University of Florida.

My Ph.D. dissertation focused on machine learning techniques for the rational design of analytic interatomic potentials. Earlier in my career, I worked in technology architecture, systems infrastructure, and quantitative finance.

That path still affects how I think about models, systems, assumptions, incentives, implementation, and failure.

Teaching

I teach courses in physics and materials science, including solid state physics, semiconductor physics, phase transitions and kinetics, thin film materials and characterization, and undergraduate physics.

My teaching usually connects standard theory to computation, and materials science concepts to fundamental physics.

I often use lectures to review material students should have seen before and to introduce mathematical tools that are useful for the problem at hand. If a model needs linear algebra, thermodynamics, probability, numerical methods, or differential equations, then those tools have to be brought back into the discussion.

My notes move between derivations, assumptions, numerical examples, code, and interpretation. The goal is to help students understand the machinery well enough to work with it, not just recognize the final formula.

Research and modeling

My primary research interest is computational materials science.

I am especially interested in the places where computational materials science intersects with other disciplines: physics, numerical methods, uncertainty quantification, machine learning, scientific software, and experimental interpretation.

Materials problems do not usually respect clean course boundaries. A useful model may need thermodynamics, quantum mechanics, crystallography, mechanics, statistics, optimization, or code that is actually maintainable.

My Ph.D. work focused on machine-learning techniques for the rational design of analytic interatomic potentials. That work still reflects the kind of problem I care about: representation, parameterization, uncertainty, and whether the computation is physically meaningful.

Software and systems

I use Python for teaching, prototyping, and computational modeling. I am also interested in building a suite of computational tools for the subjects I teach.

The purpose of my code is primarily pedagogical. There are usually other codes that are faster, more general, more optimized, or better engineered for production use. That is not the point of most of my code.

I do not want computation to become a substitute for analysis. Knowing how to operate an application is not the same thing as being computationally competent.

This connects to my interest in scientific software, numerical solvers, computational notebooks, sensor characterization, hardware-in-the-loop systems, and edge computing for physical systems.

Finance and economics

My background includes quantitative analysis, trading, and personal portfolio management.

The finance material here is mostly personal notes and work toward a private analytical system: data, models, assumptions, portfolio structure, valuation, risk, and decision workflows for a DIY investor with a technical background.

Economics enters as another modeling problem, especially through agent-based models of markets and economies. The connection to materials science is straightforward: many interacting parts, local rules, and larger-scale behavior.

Consulting and collaboration

I like working on problems that do not have clear solutions.

The kind of work that interests me usually starts with a messy technical question: the assumptions are unclear, the data are incomplete, the model is missing, or the result has to be explained well enough for someone to act on it.

That can involve modeling, computation, materials, physical systems, education, software, decision analysis, or technical strategy.
